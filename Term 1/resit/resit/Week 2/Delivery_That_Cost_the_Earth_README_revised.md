## The project

Food delivery applications have made ordering meals easier than ever. With a few taps, a meal can arrive at someone's door without them having to leave their home. However, the convenience experienced by the customer hides another part of the process: the journey made by the delivery vehicle.

Our project, *The Delivery That Cost the Earth*, explores the transport-related greenhouse-gas emissions associated with on-demand food delivery. It focuses on the contrast between fossil-fuel vehicle deliveries and bicycle deliveries, while recognising that electric vehicles, delivery distances and combined routes can produce different outcomes. The film encourages consumers to consider practical alternatives without suggesting that every motorised delivery has the same impact.

The film follows a university student ordering food through a delivery application. The viewer initially sees the convenience of the order, but the narrative then follows the delivery journey through an urban environment. It reveals the transport activity behind the seemingly simple action of ordering a meal. The film then introduces alternative choices, such as collecting food on foot when practical or selecting a lower-emission delivery option when available.

Rather than presenting climate change as a distant global problem, the film connects transport-related emissions to an everyday activity familiar to its intended audience.

**Our central message is:**

> Convenience has a footprint. Make your next delivery count.

The film should not suggest that every delivery creates an individual vehicle journey or that delivery workers are responsible for transport emissions. Instead, it encourages consumers to consider the environmental consequences of their choices while recognising that delivery platforms, restaurants, transport systems and consumers all influence the outcome.

# Problem definition

## The hidden transport impact of food delivery

On-demand food delivery has become a familiar part of urban life. Consumers can order meals through mobile applications and have them transported from restaurants to their homes. Although the customer sees a convenient digital transaction, the delivery requires a physical journey through the city.

The environmental impact of that journey depends on several factors:

- The type of vehicle used, such as a bicycle, electric bicycle, petrol-powered moped or car.
- The distance between the restaurant and customer.
- Whether the delivery is combined with other deliveries.
- The number of vehicle kilometres generated by the delivery system.
- The vehicle's energy source and associated emissions.

This means that not all food deliveries have the same environmental footprint. The London study found substantially higher per-meal emissions for car and petrol-moped deliveries than for bicycle deliveries. This should not be generalised to all motorised vehicles: electric vehicles have different operational emissions, and the result depends on the delivery system and distance.

### Evidence: the environmental difference between delivery methods

Allen et al. (2021), in their research paper *Understanding the transport and CO₂ impacts of on-demand meal deliveries: A London case study*, analysed 40,941 food deliveries undertaken by 195 delivery drivers in Greater London.

Their research found that (Allen et al., 2021):

- The average one-way journey from restaurant to customer was approximately 2.2 kilometres.
- In the study’s London dataset, car deliveries emitted approximately five times as much greenhouse gas per meal as bicycle deliveries. Petrol-moped deliveries emitted approximately eleven times as much per meal as bicycle deliveries.

These findings demonstrate that the mode of transport can make a substantial difference to the environmental impact of an individual food delivery.

Importantly, these figures come from a London case study. We use them as evidence of the relationship between delivery mode and transport emissions, not as measured emission ratios for every delivery in the Netherlands.

### The Dutch urban context

The issue also connects to the wider environmental impact of urban logistics in the Netherlands.

Kin and Quak (2025), whose paper is available through the TNO repository, report that about one-third of freight-related CO₂ emissions in the Netherlands relates to urban logistics involving vans and trucks. Their study examines alternative last-mile networks in the parcel- and home-delivery segments, including electrification, consolidation points and pickup networks. This is evidence about wider urban logistics, not a measurement of food-delivery-app emissions.

This research concerns wider urban freight and parcel-delivery systems rather than specifically measuring food-delivery applications. Nevertheless, it provides important Dutch context: the way goods are transported through cities has consequences for emissions, vehicle movements and the use of urban space.

### The specific problem we address

Our film focuses on the transport emissions associated with on-demand food deliveries and the difference between motorised and lower-emission delivery methods.

The problem is not that ordering food automatically creates a separate vehicle journey. Delivery platforms can combine multiple orders, and the environmental impact depends on how the delivery network operates.

The specific issue we want to communicate is that consumers often experience delivery as a simple digital action without considering the transport system behind it. When a lower-emission alternative is available and practical, choosing it can help reduce the transport impact of the delivery.

### Why this matters

Food delivery is an everyday activity rather than an unusual environmental event. This makes it a relevant subject for climate communication because the audience can recognise the behaviour immediately.

A student may think about the meal, delivery time and price when placing an order, but may not consider whether the journey is made by bicycle, electric vehicle, petrol moped or car.

Our film makes this otherwise invisible part of the transaction visible.

**The problem in one sentence:**

International students aged 18–24 living in student accommodation or shared housing in The Hague who order food delivery at least twice a week may focus on price, convenience and arrival time without considering the transport method used for their order. This is the specific audience and behaviour our film is designed to address; the frequency threshold is our project’s audience definition, not a claim established by the cited studies.

- That the vehicle used for that journey matters.
- That some lower-emission alternatives may be available.

# Solution: The Delivery That Cost the Earth

## What the film is designed to do

The planned deliverable is a 30-second AI-generated short film that visualises the transport journey behind on-demand food delivery and communicates practical lower-emission alternatives to the defined student audience. Update this description to past tense only after the film has been completed.

The planned narrative follows one fictional student and a connected delivery journey. It starts with the familiar experience of ordering food, follows the journey that makes the delivery possible, contrasts two transport options and concludes with a practical message.

The film is intended as a short climate-awareness intervention rather than a carbon calculator, delivery application or transport-planning system.

Its value lies in making an otherwise invisible journey understandable through a short, coherent narrative.

## How the film works

The film follows one central narrative:

**Consumer choice → Restaurant order → Delivery journey → Environmental impact → Alternative choice → Message**

Instead of using unrelated climate images, the storyboard specifies a recurring student, a recognisable apartment environment, a consistent urban setting and a connected visual style. The generated shots should make it clear that every scene belongs to the same story.

# The generation workflow

## From storyboard to finished film

The workflow is designed around the shot list rather than around individual prompts written without a larger narrative.

### Step 1 — Story and visual planning

We first define:

- The intended audience.
- The environmental issue.
- The central message.
- The recurring characters.
- The visual setting.
- The narrative sequence.

A storyboard is created before generation begins.

### Step 2 — Character and environment consistency

We establish written descriptions for the student, delivery rider, apartment and city environment.

These descriptions are reused across the relevant shots. Reference conditioning or other consistency methods will be documented if they are used in the final ComfyUI workflow.

### Step 3 — ComfyUI generation

Each shot is generated using its own prompt and recorded settings.

The workflow uses the selected LTX-2.3 model within ComfyUI to generate the required video sequences.

The prompt and seed for each shot are recorded in the storyboard.

### Step 4 — Motion and refinement

The generated sequences are reviewed for:

- Character consistency.
- Visual continuity.
- Correct scene composition.
- Natural movement.
- Alignment with the voiceover.
- Accuracy of the intended visual message.

Unsuccessful generations are recorded as discarded attempts rather than hidden from the generation count.

### Step 5 — Final editing

The generated sequences are assembled into a 30-second film.

The editing stage includes:

- Shot ordering.
- Timing and transitions.
- Recorded voiceover.
- Sound effects.
- Royalty-free background music, if used.
- Source information and final message.

No stock footage or images from another image-generation tool will be inserted into the film.

### Step 6 — Export and reproducibility

The final film should be exported as an MP4. The submitted portfolio should include the actual workflow JSON and a shot-by-shot record of prompts, seeds, settings and reference images used. A workflow should only be described as reproducible after it has been reopened and tested in a compatible ComfyUI environment.

# Ethical reflection

## What are the risks of our tool and message? Who could it harm?

Our project uses AI-generated visual storytelling to communicate the environmental impact of food delivery. Although the intention is to encourage more conscious consumer choices, there are ethical risks associated with both the subject matter and the technology used to create the film.

### 1. Misrepresenting environmental impact

One of the main risks is presenting the environmental impact of food delivery as identical for every order.

The emissions associated with a delivery depend on the vehicle, distance, route, load, energy source and whether the journey is combined with other deliveries.

The London study by Allen et al. (2021) found substantial differences between bicycle, car and petrol-moped deliveries. However, those results should not be presented as universal figures for every city or delivery platform.

We address this by identifying the research as a London case study and avoiding claims that every food order creates a separate motorised journey. We also avoid presenting generated exhaust, traffic or pollution as measured evidence.

### 2. Blaming delivery workers instead of the wider system

Another important risk is making delivery riders appear responsible for climate change.

Couriers perform a service within a system designed by delivery platforms, restaurants and logistics providers. They may not control the vehicle options, delivery routes, order allocation or time pressures imposed by the platform.

Our supplied taz article, *Arbeitsbedingungen bei Lieferando: Es sind Selbstverständlichkeiten* (2022), discusses employment conditions and the equipment provided to delivery workers in Germany. It illustrates that delivery workers have their own working conditions and employment concerns, although it should not be treated as direct evidence of conditions in the Netherlands.

We therefore avoid portraying riders as the problem. The film focuses on the transport system and consumer choices rather than criticising individual workers. We also recognise that cycling is not always a practical or available option for every courier.

### 3. Consumer guilt and unequal access to alternatives

The film encourages students to collect nearby meals or choose bicycle delivery when available. However, these alternatives are not equally accessible to everyone.

Some consumers may have disabilities, illness, mobility limitations, caring responsibilities, safety concerns or other reasons why collecting food is difficult. A bicycle delivery option may also not be offered by a particular platform or restaurant.

A message that simply tells everyone to stop ordering food would ignore these differences and could create unnecessary guilt.

We address this by using conditional language such as "when practical" and "when available". The message encourages consideration rather than presenting delivery as inherently irresponsible.

### 4. Misleading AI-generated footage

Photorealistic AI-generated scenes can be mistaken for documentary footage.

Our film depicts fictional students, couriers, restaurants and urban environments. These scenes are visual interpretations of the delivery process and do not represent actual recorded deliveries or specific measured locations.

To reduce this risk:

- We use fictional characters rather than reproducing identifiable real people.
- We avoid platform logos and identifiable company branding.
- We disclose the use of AI-generated visuals in the README and YouTube description.
- We identify the research source separately from the generated scenes.
- We avoid presenting generated pollution effects as scientific measurements.

The film should be understood as a visual awareness project, not a documentary or a scientific emissions simulation.

### 5. Consent, identity and ownership

Generative AI raises questions about the use of people's likenesses, voices and creative work.

We do not intentionally generate identifiable real people or reproduce the likeness of a specific delivery worker. The characters are fictional.

The voiceover will be recorded by a team member rather than generated by an unauthorised imitation of another person's voice. We will also ensure that any music used is royalty-free and appropriately credited.

We recognise that this does not resolve every broader question about the training data used by generative AI models, but we can avoid deliberately impersonating individuals or misrepresenting their participation.

### 6. Environmental cost of AI generation

An important contradiction in this project is that we use computationally intensive AI generation to communicate a climate-action message.

Generating images and video requires computational resources and electricity. Repeatedly generating clips to achieve a particular aesthetic can therefore create an additional environmental footprint.

We will document:

- The total number of video generations.
- The number of discarded attempts.
- The number of final shots.
- The main reasons for regenerating scenes.
- The generation environment and any available energy information.

### 7. Limitations of the message

A 30-second film cannot explain the complete environmental impact of urban logistics.

It does not calculate the emissions of an individual order, account for every vehicle type or compare the full life-cycle impact of food production, packaging and delivery.

Its scope is deliberately narrower: the relationship between delivery method and transport-related emissions.

We therefore present the film as an awareness intervention rather than a complete solution to food-delivery emissions.

### Overall ethical position

The main ethical responsibility of this project is to communicate accurately without exaggerating the environmental impact of individual orders or blaming delivery workers.

We use a London research study as a clearly identified evidence source, create fictional rather than documentary footage, disclose the use of AI, acknowledge unequal access to alternatives and record the computational effort involved in production.

We want the film to encourage informed decisions, not guilt or misinformation.

We also learned that a film requires more than visually impressive generated scenes. Continuity, character consistency, pacing, sound and the relationship between narration and visuals are essential to making the story understandable.

Researching food-delivery emissions showed us that seemingly small differences in transport choices can have significant consequences, but that these differences must be communicated with the correct context.

Finally, the project made us consider the responsibility involved in using AI to communicate environmental issues. AI-generated visuals can make an invisible process easier to understand, but they can also mislead viewers if they are presented without context.

# How to run the project

## Requirements

The workflow requires:

- A compatible, up-to-date ComfyUI installation (or ComfyUI Cloud).
- The exact LTX-2.3 model files and supporting components referenced by the workflow.
- Any additional nodes required by the exported graph.
- Sufficient GPU memory, system memory and storage for the chosen generation settings.

Do not promise that it will run on every Mac. Reproduction depends on the available hardware or cloud environment, the ComfyUI version and the model requirements. Record the environment in which you successfully tested it.

### Installation and execution

1. Install or open a compatible ComfyUI environment using the official getting-started guide: https://docs.comfy.org/get_started

2. Update ComfyUI to a version that supports the LTX-2.3 workflow you used.

3. Download the model files required by that workflow and install any additional nodes only if the workflow reports that they are missing.

4. Open ComfyUI.

5. Load the exported workflow:

   `workflow/delivery-film-workflow.json`

6. Select the desired shot configuration.

7. Check that the prompt, seed, resolution and generation settings match the storyboard.

8. Queue the workflow and wait for the video-generation process to finish.

9. Save the generated video sequence.

10. Repeat for the remaining shots and assemble the final film using the documented editing sequence.

**Important:** The workflow JSON does not contain the model weights. Anyone reproducing the project must obtain the required model files and install any additional dependencies separately. List the exact versions and download locations that were used.

**Project conclusion**

*The Delivery That Cost the Earth* uses a familiar behaviour within a clearly defined student audience to communicate a specific climate issue: the transport-related emissions associated with on-demand food delivery.

By following one order from the customer's phone through the urban delivery journey, and then showing a lower-emission alternative, the film aims to make the hidden environmental cost of convenience visible.

Our goal is not to tell people that they should never order food. It is to encourage them to think about the journey behind the order and consider a different choice when it is practical.

**Convenience has a footprint. Make your next delivery count.**
